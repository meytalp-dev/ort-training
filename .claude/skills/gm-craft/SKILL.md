# GM Craft — Narrative Storytelling

Advanced narrative design techniques for interactive storytelling. Borrowed from tabletop RPG game mastering, adapted for digital games and branching narratives.

## When to Use

- Writing branching dialogue for NPCs
- Designing narrative consequences for player choices
- Building tension and pacing in game scenes
- Creating memorable NPCs with depth
- Handling player agency vs. authored story

## Core Principles

### 1. Fail Forward

**Never let failure be a dead end.** When a player fails:

- They get NEW information (not just "wrong, try again")
- The story moves forward, but differently
- Failure creates a new, interesting problem

**Example:**
- BAD: "Wrong code. Try again." (dead end)
- GOOD: "The wrong code triggers an alarm. Now you have 60 seconds AND guards are coming." (fail forward)

### 2. Succeed at Cost

**Success should sometimes come with a price.** This creates:
- Meaningful decisions (is it worth it?)
- Dramatic tension
- Character-defining moments

**Formula:** `Success + Complication > Pure Success`

**Example:**
- FLAT: "You opened the door. Next room."
- RICH: "You opened the door, but the mechanism broke. No going back now."

### 3. NPC Motivation-First Design

**Every NPC needs a WANT and a FEAR before they get dialogue.**

| Field | Question | Example |
|-------|----------|---------|
| Want | What do they desire? | To be left alone |
| Fear | What terrifies them? | Being seen as weak |
| Method | How do they pursue their want? | Pushing people away |
| Secret | What don't they show? | They're desperately lonely |
| Trigger | What makes them snap? | Being pitied |

**Then write dialogue.** Every line should serve their want or protect against their fear.

**Test:** Cover the character name. Can you tell who's speaking? If not, the voice isn't distinct enough.

### 4. The "Yes, and..." Principle

Borrowed from improv. When a player does something unexpected:

- **"Yes, and..."** — Accept and build on it
- **"Yes, but..."** — Accept with a complication
- **Never "No."** — Block kills agency

**In code:** Always have a response for unexpected player behavior. Even if it's a gentle redirect, never ignore player input.

### 5. Scene Pacing — The Breathing Pattern

Alternate between HIGH tension and LOW tension:

```
HIGH → LOW → HIGH → LOW → CLIMAX → RESOLUTION
```

**High tension:** Timer, conflict, danger, surprise
**Low tension:** Exploration, conversation, beauty, humor

**Never:** 3+ high scenes in a row (exhaustion) or 3+ low scenes (boredom)

## Branching Narrative Architecture

### The Diamond Pattern

Don't write a tree (exponential paths). Write a diamond:

```
    A
   / \
  B   C
   \ /
    D
   / \
  E   F
   \ /
    G
```

- Choices DIVERGE then CONVERGE
- Player feels agency, author maintains control
- Key: The EXPERIENCE of B vs C must feel different, even if they lead to the same D

### Meaningful vs. Cosmetic Choices

| Type | Player perception | Actual impact | When to use |
|------|------------------|---------------|-------------|
| Meaningful | "This matters" | Changes story/gameplay | Sparingly (3-5 per session) |
| Cosmetic | "I chose this" | Same outcome, different flavor | Frequently (every dialogue) |
| Invisible | Doesn't notice | Tracks behavior for later | Always running |

**Rule:** Players need 1 meaningful choice per 5 minutes to feel agency. Fill the gaps with cosmetic choices.

### The "Remember This" Technique

From Telltale Games. When a player makes a significant choice:

1. Show a toast: "X will remember this"
2. Log the choice internally
3. Reference it LATER (even 2+ scenes later)
4. The callback can be small — a line of dialogue, a different greeting

**Impact:** Players feel their choices matter, even when the main plot doesn't change.

## Dialogue Writing Rules

1. **Max 3 lines before player input** — More than 3 = monologue = boring
2. **Every response option should be appealing** — No obvious "right answer"
3. **Silence is a choice** — If the player doesn't respond, that's a response
4. **Subtext > text** — Characters rarely say exactly what they mean
5. **Read it aloud** — If it sounds like a textbook, rewrite it
6. **No exposition dumps** — Reveal through action, not explanation

## Emotional Beats

For games dealing with emotions (SEL, therapeutic, narrative):

| Technique | How it works | When to use |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| Mirror | NPC reflects player's choice back | After a meaningful decision |
| Validate | NPC acknowledges difficulty | When player struggles |
| Challenge | NPC gently pushes back | When player avoids |
| Surprise | Unexpected reaction | To break patterns |
| Silence | NPC says nothing, just... is there | Peak emotional moments |

**The 30/60/10 Rule:**
- 30% narrative (story, dialogue, context)
- 60% action (gameplay, puzzles, exploration)
- 10% reflection (quiet moments, callbacks, meaning-making)
